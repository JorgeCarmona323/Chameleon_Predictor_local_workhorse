Dear Dr. Navia,

I'm an undergraduate researcher at San Diego State University working in the Hu Lab, with computational resources from the Jinich Lab at UC San Diego. I'm writing because your 1996 *Drug Discovery Today* paper is the direct intellectual foundation of my current project, and I'd love 20 minutes of your time to understand the scientific climate that produced it.

The project is called Chameleon Predictor. The core idea is to test your hydrophilic collapse hypothesis at scale: we use CREST conformational sampling in implicit CHCl₃ and water to compute ΔPSA (aqueous PSA − membrane PSA) across ~3,000 cyclic peptides with experimental PAMPA permeability from the CREMP dataset, then train ML models to ask whether ΔPSA predicts permeability better than conventional fingerprints. Early results suggest it does, particularly when tested across lab boundaries — which standard Morgan fingerprint models fail badly.

What you described in 1996 as computationally intractable — sampling the conformational switch in a simulation of reasonable length — is essentially what CREST was built to do. Whether it's capturing the right physics is one of the things I'd genuinely like your perspective on.

I'd love to ask you:
- What observations before the CsA NMR data made you suspect conformational switching was real?
- Why do you think the field didn't pursue this more aggressively after 1996?
- What do you make of the CREST-based approach to operationalizing the hypothesis?

I'm happy to share our preliminary results beforehand if that would make the conversation more useful.

Thank you for considering it.
